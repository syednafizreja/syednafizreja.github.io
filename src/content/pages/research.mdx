## The direction

My field is computational design and mechanics of structures: finding efficient forms through form-finding and optimisation, and checking them with finite-element analysis, buckling and seismic design. Embodied carbon is the measure I use to judge the outcome.

<More label="See why a lighter floor is not the whole answer">

Concrete shells are my current test case. They carry load through their shape rather than their thickness, so the link between form, mechanics and material is direct. In the frame I designed for my thesis, the floors hold more than half of the cradle-to-gate carbon of the building, so a lighter floor matters. In a seismic region, though, mass and stiffness decide the earthquake forces, and the design codes add minimum thicknesses, ties and diaphragm checks. I want to know when the material saving of a shell survives all of that, and when it does not.

</More>

## Three threads

[ShellBench](/work/shellbench) carries a shell floor through a whole seismic frame. [ShellForm](/work/shellform) asks whether seismic form-finding saves carbon. [The ribs study](/work/stress-line-ribs) holds the concrete volume fixed, and repeats that comparison across a volume range. [Seismic shell floors](/work/seismic-shell-floors/) has a first bay result and no carbon optimum yet. Two further threads cover durability, and whether a result can be checked.

<More label="See the three threads">

**Form and mechanics under seismic loading.** [ShellBench](/work/shellbench) follows thin-shell floors through the whole design of GB 50010 / GB 50011 reinforced concrete frames, from floor to beams, columns and lateral system, and counts the A1–A3 carbon of the whole building. In 32 of 33 buildings the shell floor lowers whole-building carbon, by a median of 5.7%, against 14.3% for the floor alone. [ShellForm](/work/shellform) asks the same question of form-found shell roofs: does including seismic loads in form-finding save carbon over thickening a gravity-only shape? Under its pre-registered 5% rule, the two strategies are not meaningfully different in most of the design space, because buckling and minimum thickness govern, not the earthquake. [Ribs against an optimised thickness](/work/stress-line-ribs) holds the concrete volume fixed on one shell: stress-line ribs fall below the plain shell, and an optimised thickness field does not. A geometric imperfection shrinks that lead. The stress-line ribs stay below the plain shell from 1.1 to 1.5 times that volume, and the order of the seven designs is the same at 0.15 g and at 0.30 g. [Seismic shell floors](/work/seismic-shell-floors/) is the next study. One smooth bay and one rib cross are both below a flat plate of equal volume. The code is not released, and there is no whole-building carbon result.

**Durability as a carbon variable.** In pavements, the larger part of the carbon benefit of a better material can come from a longer service life rather than lower production emissions. [PaveCarbon](/work/pavecarbon) carries a laboratory durability gain through a finite-element model to an annualised carbon figure.

**Results that can be checked.** The released projects are open code with automated tests, benchmarks against closed-form or published solutions, tagged releases and, where the work is a study, a pre-registered analysis plan. My own [bachelor thesis](/work/thesis-rc-frame) is the first validation case, and its errors are logged rather than hidden. The floor study is not released yet.

</More>

## What I want to do next

I am applying for funded MSc and PhD positions starting in September 2027, in computational design and mechanics of structures, including shell structures, earthquake engineering and low-carbon design. The questions I would like to work on next are form-finding and optimisation that treat seismic demand and carbon together, machine-learning surrogates that are honest about their uncertainty, and lightweight structures for extreme environments.
