## The direction

My field is computational mechanics of materials and structures: constitutive modelling, finite-element analysis, form-finding and optimisation, and seismic design, with embodied carbon as the measure I use to judge an outcome. The work moves along two arcs that meet in the middle, a materials arc that began in industry with nickel-titanium alloy components and a structures arc that began in a hand-designed seismic frame, and every number is checked before it is trusted.

<More label="See why a lighter floor is not the whole answer">

Concrete shells are my current test case. They carry load through their shape rather than their thickness, so the link between form, mechanics and material is direct. In the frame I designed for my thesis, the floors hold more than half of the cradle-to-gate carbon of the building, so a lighter floor matters. In a seismic region, though, mass and stiffness decide the earthquake forces, and the design codes add minimum thicknesses, ties and diaphragm checks. I want to know when the material saving of a shell survives all of that, and when it does not.

## Three research pillars

**1. Nonlinear constitutive and seismic mechanics.** [Diagrid seismic opt](/work/diagrid-seismic-opt) models a superelastic NiTi self-centring fuse as a one-dimensional Auricchio–Sacco constitutive driver. It is an exact tangent with Clausius–Duhem dissipation monitored at every step, set against a yielding steel fuse in a 0.40 g OpenSeesPy nonlinear time-history, then trades peak drift, residual drift and lifecycle carbon through a grouped-cross-validation Gaussian-process surrogate and NSGA-II optimisation. The honest result is a trade-off: NiTi cuts residual drift but raises peak drift and carbon unless the optimisation reallocates stiffness, so material and structure are solved together. This pillar began in industry. Two and a half years testing nickel-titanium components for durability and failure modes taught me the alloy before I modelled it, and the diagrid study is where that experience becomes a constitutive problem rather than an exotic material.

**2. Geometry, stability and low-carbon shells.** [ShellBench](/work/shellbench) follows thin-shell floors through a whole GB 50010 / GB 50011 seismic frame and counts the A1–A3 carbon of the building: in 32 of 33 buildings the shell floor lowers whole-building carbon, by a median of 5.7%. [ShellForm](/work/shellform) asks whether including seismic loads in form-finding saves carbon; under its pre-registered 5% rule the two strategies are not meaningfully different in most of the design space, because buckling and minimum thickness govern. [The ribs study](/work/stress-line-ribs) holds the concrete volume fixed and shows stress-line ribs fall below the plain shell while an optimised thickness field does not; a geometric imperfection shrinks that lead. [Seismic shell floors](/work/seismic-shell-floors/) is the next, still unreleased, study.

**3. Materials durability and lifecycle modelling.** In pavements, the larger part of a better material's carbon benefit can come from a longer service life rather than lower production emissions. [PaveCarbon](/work/pavecarbon) carries a laboratory durability gain through an axisymmetric finite-element model and mechanistic-empirical damage rules to an annualised A1–A3 carbon figure, with a Monte Carlo uncertainty check. The same durability-and-carbon discipline runs through the collaborative BIM and nano-asphalt work at Hohai.

Taken together the three pillars converge on the ground shared by solid mechanics and low-carbon construction: honest constitutive models with uncertainty-quantified surrogates, stability and seismic sizing that treat embodied carbon as a coupled variable, and results that can be checked: open code with automated tests, benchmarks against closed-form or published solutions, and tagged releases. My own [bachelor thesis](/work/thesis-rc-frame) is the first validation case, and its errors are logged rather than hidden.

<More label="See the checks behind each pillar">

**Pillar 1: nonlinear constitutive and seismic mechanics.** The diagrid constitutive driver is validated against central finite differences to under 1.9 × 10⁻¹⁰ relative, far inside the 1e-7 gate, with dissipation held within the Clausius–Duhem bound. The nonlinear time-history uses elastic beam-column members with P-Delta transforms and corotational truss fuses; the grouped-cross-validation Matérn-5/2 Gaussian-process surrogate reaches a 0% held-out false-safe rate at the 95% upper confidence bound, and the eight NSGA-II Pareto designs are re-run exactly in OpenSeesPy. A differentiable PyTorch model recovers all six NiTi parameters from 3% noisy data to 7.28% worst case.

**Pillar 2: geometry, stability and low-carbon shells.** The ribs study is pre-registered: before any result, stress-line ribs had to recover at least 70% of the optimised thickness's gain, and the orthogonal grid recovers 62.36% of it, a rare, honest negative result. ShellBench's surrogate fell short of its pre-registered coverage target and did not beat the simple rule of always choosing the shell; the verdict stays frozen in the repository.

**Pillar 3: materials durability and lifecycle modelling.** PaveCarbon's axisymmetric stresses match the Boussinesq closed form within 0.3%, the mesh is doubled to 5,120 elements, and the carbon comparison runs 1,500 Monte Carlo samples to keep the uncertainty honest. The same discipline runs through the collaborative BIM and nano-asphalt work at Hohai.

## What I want to do next

I am applying for funded MSc and PhD positions starting in September 2027, in the mechanics of materials and structures, the ground shared by solid mechanics and sustainable, low-carbon construction. The questions I would like to work on next are constitutive models for shape-memory and structural alloys with honest, uncertainty-quantified surrogates; form-finding and optimisation that treat seismic demand and embodied carbon together; and lightweight structures for extreme environments.
